Gas Fingerprint Key

Every glowing gas emits its own exact set of lines. Match the pattern you see, not just one color.
HydrogenH · lines at 656, 486, 434, 410 nm
Notice: four clean, well-spaced lines with one strong red. The exact set from the lecture: 656, 486, 434, 410 nm.
HeliumHe · brightest line 588 nm
Notice: one blazing yellow line near 588 nm that hydrogen does not have, plus blue and red companions.
NeonNe · dense lines 585-703 nm
Notice: a crowded picket fence of orange and red lines. Nothing else looks this busy on the warm end.
NitrogenN₂ · fuzzy bands, not sharp lines
Notice: smeared bands instead of sharp lines: violet-blue on one end, orange-red on the other. The tube glows pink-orange overall.
XenonXe · blue cluster 450-500 nm
Notice: a tight cluster of blue lines and almost nothing warm. The tube looks pale blue-lavender.
MercuryHg · 405, 436, 546, 577+579 nm
Notice: sparse and cool: one violet, one strong blue, one strong green, and a tight yellow pair. No red at all.

Reading Starlight · Key 2 of 2

Sky Survey Decoder

You identified the gas. Now find out what you were actually looking at.
Fingerprint you foundWhat you were looking atThe story
Hydrogen Proxima Centauri The nearest star to our Sun, a small red dwarf 4.2 light-years away. Like almost every star, it is overwhelmingly hydrogen. Its light left the star over four years before it reached your eyes tonight.
Helium The Sun In 1868, astronomers studying the Sun's light during an eclipse found a bright yellow line near 588 nm that matched no element known on Earth. They had discovered a new element inside a star, 93 million miles away, using only its fingerprint. They named it helium, after helios, the Greek word for Sun.
Neon The Cat's Eye Nebula A dying star that has blown off its outer layers into glowing shells of gas. The neon in those shells was forged inside the star itself. Stars are element factories, and this is what one looks like at the end.
Nitrogen The aurora, Earth's own sky Not a star. Charged particles streaming off the Sun slam into the nitrogen in our upper atmosphere and make it glow, exactly like the gas in a spectrum tube. The northern lights are a planet-sized discharge tube.
Xenon Stadium floodlights Caught you. This light never came from space: xenon arc lamps light up stadiums and IMAX projectors. Astronomers must recognize and filter out human-made light like this every single night.
Mercury A parking-lot streetlight Fooled again. Mercury-vapor lamps light streets and gyms, and their glow washes out the night sky. Light pollution is such a problem that observatories are built on remote mountaintops to escape fingerprints like this one.
Careful: not everything glowing in the night sky is a star. Two of the lights in this survey were made by people. Your fingerprint evidence is the only way to tell.
